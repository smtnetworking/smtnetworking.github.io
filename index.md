# SMT-V: The Society for Music Theory Videocast Journal

_SMT-V_ is the open-access, peer-reviewed video journal of the [Society for Music Theory](http://www.societymusictheory.org). Founded in 2014, _SMT-V_ publishes video essays that showcase research in music theory in a dynamic, audiovisual format, presented so as to have the potential to engage both specialists within the field as well as interested viewers outside the music theory community. The journal features a supportive and collaborative production process, and publishes several videos each year. [Read more about _SMT-V_ here.](about)

### "Singing Ut-Re-Mi with Palestrina: How Solmization Shapes Counterpoint"
Megan Kaes Long (Oberlin College Conservatory of Music)

*[Link to bibliography](http://www.smt-v.org/bibliographies/12_6_Long.pdf)*

*In the sixteenth century, young musicians were trained to sight-read and think about music in much the same way that music students learn today: using solmization, the precursor to our modern* do–re–mi *system. The version of solmization that was developed in the Middle Ages and Renaissance differed in some key ways from modern solmization, but had many of the same advantages for developing musicians: it taught them how to read staff notation, how to sing intervals correctly, and how to understand the relationship of the notes they were singing to some overarching tonal organization. In contemporary moveable-do solmization—the version that most Anglophone musicians know—solmization syllables teach musicians how each note in the scale relates back to the tonic of a major or minor key. In the Renaissance, solmization taught musicians a different set of relationships. This video-essay introduces viewers to Renaissance solmization and uses examples from Palestrina’s best-known works to explore the kinds of contrapuntal and tonal relationships that Renaissance solmization made its practitioners attentive to. It shows how hexachordal solmization helps us understand the tonal implications of Palestrina’s motivic transpositions, and trains us to listen for softening (moving flatter) and hardening (moving sharper) in sixteenth-century points of imitation.*

***Keywords**: hexachord, solmization, Guido of Arezzo, Palestrina, modality, tonality, notation, [history of theory](https://www.smt-v.org/teach/history.html), [counterpoint](https://www.smt-v.org/teach/counterpoint.html)*
